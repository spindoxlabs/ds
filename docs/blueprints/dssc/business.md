## Business Model

> **Source** · DSSC Blueprint v3.0 › Business and Organisational Building Blocks › Business Model

> **Ambiguous:** the *Business Model* page carries no breadcrumb trail in the source's own index, so its position within the pane's three families (Business / Governance / Legal Building Blocks) cannot be determined from the source. The pane navigation lists it first, ahead of *Use Case Development*. Its own page heading is a slug (`business-model`); the name used here, **Business Model**, is the one the pane navigation gives it.

The building block holds that whether a data space is funded publicly or privately, and whether it has a for-profit or not-for-profit orientation, it must have a business model that speaks to its long-term financial sustainability. A data space's business model differs from that of a data-sharing platform: it requires collaboration between multiple actors, balancing economic viability, governance, and trust. The building block provides a structured approach to designing and evolving such business models.

### Scope and objectives

The building block is composed of four elements:

| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| The value proposition | To develop a viable business model, you need to differentiate a data space from other data-sharing platforms or even peer-to-peer data sharing. While each data space will have a unique proposition, they likely share three commonalities: they provide efficiency gains; they improve interoperability; they increase sovereignty and control. |
| A business model template | The value proposition is only one element of the business model, which needs to address questions around use cases, customers, providers, and governance. A business model template can be a useful tool to structure discussion and thoughts around the specifics of the business model. |
| A revenue model | The revenue model describes how to monetise the value created through data sharing, access, and services. It specifies who pays whom, for what type of data or service (e.g. access, usage, value-added processing), and under which conditions. These revenues can come from both the public and private sector. |
| A growth pathway | As data spaces grow, their business models also evolve. The business model of the data space should be monitored, reviewed, and adapted regularly to maximise its potential for growth and sustainable expansion. |

Questions the building block answers: how the business model of a data space is distinct from a data-sharing platform; what unique selling points differentiate it; what potential revenue models exist; and what templates can be used to construct it.

### What are the unique elements?

Data spaces share characteristics with data-sharing platforms, but what makes them unique is that they are not controlled by a single organisation, and data resides with the data owners themselves. A data space provides the connectors and services but never takes control of the data itself. Because no one organisation controls a data space, developing its business model is a collective exercise between its founding members, who form a governance authority. This makes strategic decision-making around scope and pricing more complex and prone to compromise that could jeopardise long-term viability; a cohesive governance authority with aligned interests is essential.

### The value proposition

Data spaces create value in three ways:

- **Efficiency gains**: Reduce economic transaction costs by standardising interfaces and lowering integration efforts, especially across sectors and ecosystems.
- **Improved collaboration**: Facilitate collaboration between data providers and consumers, which can improve interoperability.
- **Sovereignty & control**: Ensure data owners maintain control over access and usage conditions, enhancing trust. This includes avoiding lock-in.

On *efficiency gains*: data spaces provide standardised interfaces and connectors so developers no longer need to develop specific interfaces for specific data sets; with sufficient data sources connected, search costs fall; where conditions of data usage are pre-specified and participants are verified, bargaining and contracting costs fall too. On *improved collaboration*: a governance authority coordinates the availability of data space offerings, federation and participant agent services, and value-creation services either in-house, outsourced to data space intermediaries, or via a decentralised approach. On *sovereignty & control*: data spaces provide connectors and do not centralise data on their own servers; data is never copied from one location to another.

### Building out the business model

A business model template supports development of an initial business model, reflecting participants, service providers, and members of the governance authority, as well as partners. Materials from other parts of the blueprint feed the canvas:

| Questions to consider | Building block |
|---|---|
| What are the promising ideas on which the data space will be built? | Use case development |
| What data is available and what services can make that data more useful and attractive? | Data space offerings |
| In which context and with whom is the data space being explored? | Organisational form and governance authority |
| Are there existing networks in which stakeholders already interact and by what rules are they already engaging with each other? | Participation management |
| What are the relevant legislation and developments in the domain for the data space? | Regulatory compliance |

Questions explicitly placed **outside the scope of the blueprint**: whether existing digital infrastructure can be drawn on; the maturity level of the organisations involved; and where financing for development and operational phases comes from.

The template's elements, in the suggested order in which the canvas can be filled:

| # | Element of template | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Principles, Scope, Objectives | What is the thematic scope of the data space? What are its objectives, growth aspirations, and profit goals? These largely determine the types of use cases and participants to engage with, as well as the effort required to promote one side or the other. |
| 2 | Use Cases, Data Providers and Data Users | Which use cases are currently in scope, and which will be in the future? Who are the data providers and data recipients in these use cases? |
| 3 | Services and Providers | Which services does the data space have and which to offer? And who is providing them? We distinguish federation services, participant agent services and value creation services. They can be supplied on behalf of the data space or provided by third parties. The governance authority may determine the number of service providers and the criteria they must meet. The different services may require different value propositions and revenue models. |
| 4a | Governance Authority Bodies and Members | Who are the participants in the representative assembly of the governance authority? Who is eligible to join? What criteria should they meet? In certain cases, the composition of the governance authority is prescribed by law; in others, the initiative can establish procedures. Who are the participants in the execution body? How does the data space ensure that the individuals comprising the governance authority continue to fulfil their responsibilities? |
| 4b | Stakeholders | Does the data space initiative need support from additional organizations, such as industry associations, governmental agencies, or non-governmental organizations? IT providers and other partners: does the data space initiative procure services from parties that are not considered data space participants? What are their principles, and how dependent is the data space upon them? |
| 5 | People, Resources and Activities | What staff, resources, and activities are deployed by the data space to maintain operations? Cost model: what operational costs does the data space incur? The main cost factors include development costs, operational costs for data space enabling services, and costs related to the governance of the data space. For the data space to sustain itself, the revenue model must cover all expenses. The revenues and costs must align with the data space's profit and growth strategies. |
| 6a | Value Proposition to Service Providers | What does the data space offer to service providers? Services are part of the value proposition to attract use cases, but these service providers must also be drawn to the data space to deliver their services. Access to a large user base can be an attractor. |
| 6b | Value Proposition to Use Cases, Data Providers and Data Recipients | How does the data space aim to help identify, attract, onboard, and develop use cases? Enabling services and value-added services are also of value to use cases. |
| 6c | Revenue and Pricing (for Use Cases, Data Providers, Data Recipients) | How does the data space initiative generate revenue from use cases, and what pricing strategy is used? The income may originate from multiple sources, including public funding (project-based or ongoing subsidies) and participant fees (e.g. subscriptions, memberships, or transaction fees). The revenue model combines the different revenue mechanisms upon which the data space depends. |
| 6d | Revenue and Pricing (to Service Providers) | How does the data space initiative build revenue from service providers, and what pricing strategy is used? |
| 7 | Network Effects | How does the data space aim to establish critical mass in use cases, data providers, data participants, and service providers? How does it aim to establish same-side and cross-side network effects? Establishing critical mass and network effects can be guided by temporary pricing and other incentives. |
| 8 | Dynamic Capabilities | Monitoring: how does the data space keep track of necessary changes in the business model? Business Model Innovation Process: how does the data space identify the need to change its business model, redesign it, and implement these desired changes? Governance: how is the data space governed? |

### The revenue model

Revenue and the term 'business model' are sometimes interpreted as concepts exclusive to for-profit organisations; however, any data space must understand its income, revenue, and cost structure. The listed revenue models are not exclusive and in some cases can be mixed and matched.

| Revenue category | Specific model | What is paid for | Best used when | Risk | Freemium option |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Membership & access | Basic subscription fees, tiered by organisation size and role | Right to join and participate in the data space | Stable governance authority with clear coordination value | May discourage high-value contributors if subscription fees are set too high | Yes (free tier for small organizations or trial periods) |
| Membership & access | Accreditation or certification fees | Compliance and trust labels | Operating in regulated or high-trust environments | Limited revenue potential due to one-time or infrequent payments | No |
| Membership & access | Premium ecosystem access | Access to curated networks of service providers, advanced tools, and matchmaking services | Large data space with diverse, high-quality participant network | Requires significant scale to be viable | Yes (basic access free, premium features paid) |
| Usage-based | Transaction fees | Each data exchange or contract executed | Mature data space with high levels of active exchange activity | May discourage early-stage experimentation and adoption | Yes (limited free transactions) |
| Usage-based | Volume-based fees | Data volume transferred, frequency of exchanges, or connection time | Recovering operational costs of infrastructure | Pricing may not reflect actual business value created | Yes (free usage quotas) |
| Value-based | Commission-based pricing | Outcomes achieved or risks mitigated through data use | Clear value can be attributed to specific data exchanges | Complex to measure and enforce value attribution | No |
| Value-based | Revenue sharing | Percentage of downstream revenue generated | Commercial ecosystems with monetizable outputs | Difficult to track data usage and collect payments downstream | No |
| Service-layer | Onboarding and integration fees | Technical setup, legal agreements, and data structure alignment | Early phases of data space development or low-trust environments | Not scalable as primary revenue source long-term | Yes (basic onboarding free, complex integrations paid) |
| Service-layer | Managed services | Quality assurance, Service Level Agreements (SLAs), identity management, and trust services | Participants lack internal operational capacity | May undermine perceived neutrality of the data space | Yes (self-service free, managed options paid) |
| Governance & institutional | Arbitration & rule services | Dispute resolution and rule interpretation | Contested environments or heavily regulated sectors | Revenue is unpredictable and difficult to sustain profitably | No |
| Governance & institutional | Standard stewardship | Maintaining data formats and exchange standards | Fragmented or rapidly evolving technical domains | May be perceived as a "tax" if value is not clearly communicated | No |
| Public funding | Operational government funding | Continuous operation of public data infrastructure | Public-good or regulatory mandated domains | Creates political and budgetary dependency | n/a |
| Public funding | Procurement contracts | Service provision against Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) | Clear service requirements can be defined | Risk of vendor lock-in | n/a |
| Public funding | Programme-based funding | Delivery of defined public outcomes | Multi-year policy initiatives with stable objectives | Limited flexibility to adapt scope | n/a |
| Compliance driven | Regulatory mandate funding | Industry-funded infrastructure to meet government compliance requirements | Compliance-heavy sectors where regulation mandates data sharing or reporting (e.g. finance, healthcare, environmental) | Risk of minimum-viable approach; potential pushback on costs; over-specification of requirements | No |

### Evolving the business model

The business model should be monitored, reviewed, and adapted regularly. The source gives two illustrative development pathways, which are examples rather than requirements:

- **A sample development pathway from SCSN** — Initial Context (proprietary service providers facilitating ERP interconnectivity); Feasibility Research; Standard Establishment; Governance Formation (a governing body created; federation services transferred from a research institute to an external IT service provider); Platform User Benefits; Current Challenges (increasing interactions and expanding use cases).
- **A sample development pathway from DjustConnect** — Proof of Concept; Vision Development; Dual Strategy Implementation; Revenue Model Establishment (a fixed fee per farmer, initially paid by cooperatives, with a core value proposition of managing data-sharing authorisations); Governance Evolution (from a foundation to a cooperative structure); Refinement and Expansion.

## Example Canvases

> **Source** · DSSC Blueprint v3.0 › Business and Organisational Building Blocks › Example Canvases

> **Ambiguous:** this page also carries no breadcrumb trail in the source's index and its own heading is a slug (`example-canvases`). The pane navigation lists it as a separate entry immediately after *Business Model*; the *Business Model* page links to it as "the examples page". Whether upstream treats it as a sub-page of *Business Model* or as a sibling cannot be determined from the source.

This page is **illustration, not requirement**. It fills the business model template for two real data spaces. No requirement rows are generated from it.

**Example 1: Smart Connected Supplier Network (SCSN)** — Principles: sovereignty (self-determination of who will receive data), safe exchange of data, increased efficiency in supply chains. Scope: supply chain in the manufacturing industry; invoice, order, product information data. Objectives: 20% higher productivity of the supply chain, expansion horizontally and vertically in the value chain initially on a European scale, no profit ambitions for the SCSN foundation. One use case currently: connecting ERP and other order systems. Interoperability is established through a jointly developed standard whose maintenance is the core task of the SCSN foundation. Use case participants pay fees to service providers, not directly to the data space. The executive body arranges a broker service and identity provisioning (outsourced to KPN), which also provides a vocabulary hub. The SCSN foundation gets a predetermined fixed contribution per service provider, amended by a fee per end user. Governance: one representative each from the service providers, the manufacturing industry, a knowledge institute and an industry association; at least one director; three governance documents — Statutes or Articles of Association of SCSN, Accession Agreement, Code of Conduct. There is no structural process in place to evaluate and monitor, or to innovate, the business model.

**Example 2: Djustconnect** — Principles: respect for ownership and decision-making power; transparency about who requests and receives data; trust is earned by only exchanging data with permission. Scope: agricultural sector in Flanders. Currently 36 applications for farmers. The data space initiative does not actively support the development of new use cases. The governance authority receives an annual fixed fee from data providers and data receivers; farmers' data is held at no cost to the farmer. ILVO provides the connectors on behalf of Djustconnect — API management, API market, access control, authorization, a vocabulary hub, data authorization, and a clearing house — all hosted in-house. EV ILVO owns Djustconnect as a non-profit, founded by a number of associations and subsidised with EFRO funding. A Steering Committee of Cooperatives ensures farmer representation; an External Advisory Board represents the wider ecosystem. Governance documents: Code of Conduct (based on the European Code of Conduct for data sharing in agriculture), User Agreement, Privacy Statement. There are no structural processes in place to evaluate, monitor or innovate the business model.

### Use Case Development

> **Source** · DSSC Blueprint v3.0 › Business and Organisational Building Blocks › Business Building Blocks › Use Case Development

The value of a data space stems from its use cases. Data space use cases are settings where two or more participants create business, societal or environmental value from data sharing. Use case development amplifies such value. It is particularly important for data spaces in their early phases, as use cases attract users and participants that are essential for growth. An established data space with well-functioning use cases may opt out of use case development, but risks becoming obsolete as its business environment evolves.

#### Scope and objectives

Use case development falls into three initial steps, plus continuous improvement throughout the life cycle as the overarching process model:

| Key stages of use case development | Description |
|---|---|
| Identifying and tracking use case scenarios | Collecting ideas for use case scenarios through activities such as observing potential users' needs and analysing other data spaces and platforms. |
| Refining use case scenarios | Refining the description and design of use case scenarios using templates like the Data Cooperation Canvas, Use Case Playbook, or self-created templates. |
| Implementing use cases | Implementing use cases both from organisational and business perspectives (e.g. agreements) and from technical perspectives (e.g. vocabularies, APIs, connectors). |
| Continuous improvement process | Continuously analyzing the performance of use cases, identifying improvement opportunities, and planning and implementing changes in a systematic and managed way throughout the life cycle of a use case. |

#### Concepts

- **Use case and use case scenario** — A data space use case is a clearly defined situation where participants exchange and use data to achieve a measurable outcome or solve a problem. The term **use case** refers to an operational use case; when referring to a planned or envisioned setting that is not yet operational, the term **use case scenario** is used. Use cases vary in complexity, from one-off transactions between two parties to complex, multi-party interactions such as the creation of a digital product passport. Complex use cases can be built with the support of multiple, simpler use cases.
- **Cross data space use case** — A use case where the data sharing extends to two or more data spaces. They may use data products and services from several data spaces or even from other data sharing infrastructures, such as platforms.
- **Use case orchestrator** — The entity that represents and is accountable for a specific use case within the governance framework. The orchestrator establishes and enforces the rules that make the use case work, and assembles and manages a network to develop the use case. This orchestrator can be the governance authority or a participant. An orchestrator is particularly necessary with many inter-related simple use cases or for complex use cases; simple transactions between a data provider and recipient can be done without orchestration.

#### Elements to develop use cases and their key functions

Use cases will generally be moderated by the governance authority as per the data space rulebook. The governance authority can act as a use case orchestrator or nominate one or more participants; these roles can be formalised or done on an ad-hoc, case-by-case basis. The question of who orchestrates is not an either-or scenario — the governance authority can take responsibility for some use cases and appoint participants to manage others.

**Questions that a use case needs to answer.** Use cases need to answer basic questions around the purpose of sharing data and what value it aims to create: the purpose and value; who uses it; why these users need it; how it fits into existing workflows or changes them; what kind of data and services are shared; who provides the data; who the participants are and what their roles and responsibilities are; what value they expect; what user journeys or lifecycle stages rely on it; what the role of personal data and natural persons in the use case is; what kind of value the use case creates for the data space and how it fits its value proposition; and what measurable KPIs or outcomes define success. Answering these questions is an iterative process; design decisions may also need to change.

**Identifying and tracking use case scenarios.** Data spaces benefit from tracking the progress of use case scenarios, recording: a short description; the scenario's development stage (Exploring; Scoping & Defining; Feasibility Assessment / Stage-gate Screening; Refining; Developing; Piloting; Implemented; Abandoned — and the reasons why); the revenue potential (from very high to very low); the strategic fit (from very high to very low); required data products and services; and potential providers of those products and services, including whether they are currently participants. In addition to tracking use cases, it also makes sense to keep track of data products and value creation services available on the market, also outside one's own data space.

**When to commit to refining a use case.** Not every scenario should be taken to the potentially laborious refinement stage; promising ideas should undergo an initial stage-gate screening. Ranking use cases in terms of strategic fit and revenue potential can help determine where to focus effort. It is also good to give some initial thought to the data and service providers needed before progressing to the refinement phase.

**Refining (complex) use case scenarios.** Where the governance authority is not the orchestrator, it can provide structured approaches and templates, which might include customised versions of: Business Model Radar; Data Space Canvas; Data Cooperation Canvas; Use Case Playbook; and Use Case Blueprint. The existing approaches share a core structure reflecting: the participants and their role or activity; the flow of data (products) and services; the value for participants; and the joint purpose or value of the use case.

**Design elements when refining use case scenarios:**

- *Modularity* — Efficiency and network effects can be improved if the data products and services are modular so that they can serve multiple use cases.
- *Cost basis* — The implementation and operational costs of individual use cases might be fully or partially covered by the data space. If use cases build on common infrastructure covered by the governance authority, sharing data products and offerings across use cases would lower overhead and make scaling easier. The viability of use cases depends on the willingness of data rights holders to share data; agreeing on compensation and fees while balancing monetary and non-monetary costs and benefits is crucial.
- *Regulation* — Regulation typically needs to be considered at an early stage. Certain characteristics of a use case may trigger legal requirements: the type of data, the nature of the participants, or the domain. Depending on the use case, different horizontal, sectoral, and national regulations may apply.
- *Existing infrastructure and participants* — A use case may require specific data models or services provided; further boundaries are set by the willingness of the use case participants to participate in the role suggested for them.
- *Contracts* — Use cases function under the general institutional agreements and governance framework of a data space. In many cases these contract types can be sufficient; however, a lawyer should examine in the refinement stage whether other agreements or contracts are necessary.
- *Security* — Some important security related regulations that use cases must comply with are, among others, **NIS2 (Network and Information Security Directive 2)**, **CRA (Cyber Resilience Act)**, and the **DSA (Digital Services Act)**. Security should always be a priority but may be particularly important in critical industries covered by the NIS2 Directive or sectors covered by other legislation. A higher security level may be required if the use case involves processing sensitive data. Threat modelling can be used to systematically identify and evaluate potential threats. For use cases where the information is considered critical, periodic security audits may be advisable, along with the necessary backups to prevent data loss.

**Implementing use cases.** Before a use case can become operational, all the preconditions need to be met. The implementation of complex use cases typically takes place in a stepwise manner; the orchestrator may start by implementing a minimum viable use case that includes only essential functionality. The use case orchestrator acts as the process owner.

**Continuous improvement process.** Continuous improvement is needed due to changes in technology, available data, and market conditions. Changes in one aspect of a use case — a data product, a value creation service, a participant, or an agreement — may have implications on the other aspects and participants. Change management is needed in all the phases of the life cycle. The more important the use case is for the data space, the more important it is to have effective change management and continuous improvement processes in place. When there are multiple changes to be made, they need to be prioritized and a roadmap made.

> *The deployEMDS stepwise implementation example (the Barcelona use case "Multi-operator data governance ecosystem for bus fleets and demand-responsive transport", Deliverable D4.2) is given as illustration; no requirements are drawn from it.*

### Data Space Offerings

> **Source** · DSSC Blueprint v3.0 › Business and Organisational Building Blocks › Business Building Blocks › Data Space Offerings

A data space offering bundles data products and services in a way that a potential consumer can interact with. This building block provides data space initiatives with an understanding of these offerings from a business perspective, proposing a strategy to develop and maintain them.

#### Scope and objectives

The elements of a data space offering strategy are: identify and onboard data products and services that serve existing and future use cases; develop, maintain, and enforce the rules that govern data space offerings; and support the participants to develop and offer high-quality data products and services.

In particular, this building block **recommends the governance authorities**: consider what data products and services their data space offers to the participants; establish governance rules for the offered data products and services; prioritise onboarding of relevant data products and services to foster network effects; and guide participants in creating and providing data products and services via use cases.

#### Concepts

**Data products.** A data product is more than just data. Turning data into a product means transforming and packaging it into something that is consumable and marketable. The **CEN CENELEC Trusted Data Transactions Workshop Agreement** defines data products as *"data sharing units, packaging data and metadata, and any associated license terms"*. The source notes that the definition of a data product is still evolving.

While the content of data products may vary, it typically includes: the data; and the metadata describing access and usage terms as well as the following aspects in a structured, machine-readable format — a plain-language description of what the data is, what it represents, and why it exists; quality, format, frequency, duration and other requirements the data product fulfills; access and control rights (e.g. allowed purposes of use, attribution, Intellectual Property Rights, liabilities, geographical limitations, usability for training AI models); delivery options (e.g. APIs, SMTP, web interface); information about data provenance and lineage; pricing and billing information; and other information (e.g. ethical considerations).

**Data space offering.** While data products can be shared and sold on their own, they can also be bundled with data services — data visualization, anonymization, data quality assessment and assurance, data processing, or connection-enabling services to external infrastructures or applications. The service can be something as simple as an API. This bundling of data with data services is a data space offering. These offerings are published by the data space participants or the governance authority in a catalogue to make them discoverable.

#### Elements of a data space offering and their key functions

**Identify and priority onboard data products and services that serve existing and future use cases.** Different use cases often require the same resources. Data spaces can accelerate the development of use cases by proactively identifying and attracting data products or services that overlap with multiple use cases. Who takes the lead — the governance authority or a participant driving a specific use case (an 'orchestrator') — depends on the data space's business model. If a data service is offered by the governance authority, a key business consideration is the cost of these data services, and whether to develop these in house or procure them from service providers.

**Develop, maintain, and enforce the rules around a data space offering.** The governance authority sets the boundaries that will govern which data products and services can be offered. These rules — including standards and guidelines — ensure the coherence of the data space's value proposition and lay out basic conditions around quality, trustworthiness, security, privacy, interoperability, and ethics. The governance authority should develop rules around:

- What types of data space offerings (requiring what kind of data products and services) can be offered through the data space, such as domain-specific data products, supporting also streaming data or AI models.
- Requirements to access data products (irrespective of the data space offerings). **Article 33 of the Data Act** requires that *"dataset content, use restrictions, licenses, data collection methodology, data quality and uncertainty shall be sufficiently described, where applicable, in a machine-readable format, to allow the recipient to find, access and use the data"*. Details on how those principles should be addressed need to be clarified by setting quality requirements, which can include: quality, utility, or ethical labels; availability conditions; the format and content requirements for human-readable descriptions; provenance data requirements; and delivery options.
- License terms specifying the access and usage rules of the data product or service, such as attribution, Intellectual Property Rights, liabilities, and geographical limitations.
- Commercial aspects, such as pricing and billing information.
- Delivery options, such as APIs, SMTP, web interface, and mapping tools.
- Supporting and maintenance services.

**Supporting participants to bring high-quality data space offerings.** The governance authority may provide tools and processes to lower the barrier for participants to start providing data space offerings. For data products, the governance authority can use long-established product management practices to plan, develop and govern the data product over its life cycle. The governance authority can also provide documentation to data product owners on the use cases, which is equally relevant to service providers.

The following key tasks are required for developing and providing data space offerings, which can be carried out iteratively:

1. **Identify the purpose** of the data product or service in the context of use cases. Data space offerings (and their components) should satisfy consumers' needs with reuse in mind, and should start on the consumers' side to clarify the expected value.
2. **Developing data products and services** — might require developing several candidates and then choosing the one that gets the best score after evaluation. When the owner defines the purpose, they need to identify the data sources that provide the data ingredients; all licensing terms, commercial aspects, delivery options, consumption pattern, information about data provenance and lineage, requirements and various policies need to be decided; the necessary metadata needs to be created using the suitable standard; and all this required information needs to be bundled into a consumable form, using a suitable standard.
3. **Quality assurance, evaluation and validation** — the data product or service needs proper testing, as well as validation that it fulfills its requirements and intended purposes.
4. **Publishing data space offerings via catalogue services** — a data space offering should be made available for consumers.
5. **Maintenance and supporting services** — the provider is responsible for maintaining and supporting services throughout the whole lifecycle.

### Intermediaries and Operators

> **Source** · DSSC Blueprint v3.0 › Business and Organisational Building Blocks › Business Building Blocks › Intermediaries and Operators

A core design choice that data spaces need to make is the configuration of service providers that allow a data space to function. While the **data space governance authority (DSGA)** and data space participants can provide these services themselves, there are many business (and governance) reasons why procuring services provides benefits. A data space can have one operator or multiple enabling service providers (intermediaries).

#### Scope and objectives

The building block helps governance authorities and designers of individual data spaces understand whether they should rely on a single operator or a series of data space intermediaries. The effectiveness of intermediaries and operators is ultimately a balance between four dimensions: (1) to streamline and make trusted data sharing easier and more economical; (2) to improve data space accessibility and usability; (3) to contribute to the data space's scalability; and (4) to enable interoperability both within and between data spaces. Risks associated with procurement of services are often related to vendor lock-in, additional costs associated with vendor management, and potential loss of sovereignty depending on the kind of provider selected.

#### Running a data space with a single operator or multiple intermediaries

An **operator model** implies that a single service provider provides the vast majority of enabling services; a data space serviced by different specialised providers uses an **intermediary model**. The most common roles of intermediaries and operators are to provide neutral and trusted federation and participant agent services, but they may also facilitate growth by easing accessibility, enable cross-data space interoperability, and offer business and organisational services. The data space governance framework (which may be documented in a rulebook) or legal frameworks may limit how enabling services may be bundled with value creation services.

**Role of the business and revenue model.** There are multiple options for revenue models that can also coexist with each other: fixed participation fees (from participant to intermediary, or from intermediary to data space); dynamic fees based on service usage; revenue sharing arrangements between the intermediary and the data space; and transaction-dependent fees (data transaction, trust transaction, financial transaction, etc.).

**Role of interoperability requirements.** Collaboration between intermediaries and operators divides into: collaboration between intermediaries providing different enabling services; collaboration between intermediaries providing the same enabling services; and collaboration between operators to facilitate interoperability between data spaces. Collaboration between intermediaries providing different services requires technical integration planning and governance for this integration. Collaboration between intermediaries providing the same services (e.g. multiple connected registry or catalogue services such as country-specific registries, or multiple credential-issuing services) requires that the different intermediaries be compatible with each other by conforming to the standards and frameworks adopted by the data space in its governance framework. A data space may recognise multiple approved providers of participant agent services and give participants the freedom to choose between competing service providers; these providers can compete on different aspects of the service offering, but must always be fungible, or interchangeable.

**Role of operator efficiencies (and operator capture).** A common concern with a single operator model is that the operator may take a too central role. Switching costs can be high, allowing operators to extract excess revenues; at the same time a single operator reduces complexity. Data space designers can reduce the risk of being 'captured' by establishing good governance, applying and enforcing standards and use of open source technologies, and designing data spaces that take into account changing operators and/or moving to an intermediary model to distribute vendor dependency risks.

#### Breaking down operator and intermediary roles for easier (business) analysis

**Data space service model characteristics.** The governance framework and the data space registry should include information regarding all service providers in the data space describing the following characteristics: type of service(s) provided (e.g. federation services, policy information point (PIP) services, participant agent service, value adding service); procurement model, i.e. whether the governance authority or individual participants pay the service provider for the use of the service; and users, i.e. whether the contractor is also the service user or whether the service is contracted by the governance authority for the use of participants.

The DSGA needs to decide whether to enforce that service providers commit to the data space rulebook and write contractual agreements on participating in the data space. Enforcing participation allows the DSGA to govern service provision and enable collaborative business models; allowing independent service providers to operate may be attractive for providers who do not want to be governed. The DSGA should evaluate the benefits and downsides, because this may have an impact on the economics, resilience and growth of the data space.

**Governance characteristics.** Intermediaries and operators are participants of a data space and as such subject to the governance framework (rulebook) of that data space. Governance-related characteristics may include: operator / intermediary status, also possibly some intermediary sub-type status such as personal data intermediary; exclusivity status; mandatory / optional status; bundling allowance; auditing requirement or possibility (e.g. the DSGA may have the right to audit service providers' technical capabilities or business accounts); business conditions (e.g. whether the provider is subject to fee or revenue-sharing obligations); and motivation for inclusion.

#### A word on regulatory compliance

Intermediaries (and operators) are subject to broader legal frameworks. Key regulatory characteristics of service providers might include the applicability of requirements imposed by: **competition law (e.g. DMA)**; **intellectual property law**; **data protection (e.g. GDPR)**; **digital services regulations (e.g. DSA, DGA, eIDAS)**; **sector-specific regulatory compliance (e.g. EHDS)**; and specific national and regional regulatory frameworks.

The **Data Governance Act (DGA)** requires Data Intermediation Service Providers (DISPs) to act as neutral intermediaries, ensuring secure, transparent, and privacy-compliant data sharing. DISPs must facilitate data sovereignty, maintain accountability, and comply with data protection laws like GDPR. Not all intermediaries fall under the applicability of the DGA — some services may not be considered a DISP when, for instance, they do not aim to establish commercial relationships for data sharing, or where they facilitate transactions between a determined number of data subjects, data holders, and users. Any DISP who intends to provide the data intermediation services referred to in **Article 10 DGA** shall submit a notification to the competent authority for data intermediation services, and shall be subject to the conditions laid down in **art. 12 DGA**.

### Organisational Form and Governance Authority

> **Source** · DSSC Blueprint v3.0 › Business and Organisational Building Blocks › Governance Building Blocks › Organisational Form and Governance Authority

This building block encompasses key decision-making points for the effective establishment and operation of a data space: the determination of an organisation's form and the establishment of a data space, the creation of a governance authority, and the creation and realisation of a data space governance framework. The choice of legal form of a data space impacts the type and role of a governance authority, the ability of a data space to develop and implement its governance framework and, ultimately, the data space's overall development and sustainability.

#### Scope and objectives

The building block describes the options of creating a data space as an **unincorporated** (i.e. without legal personhood) and an **incorporated** (i.e. as a legal person) entity, and discusses the most important consequences of these choices in a comparative way. Determining the organisational form and establishing the governance authority should be completed before the data space enters the operational phase. At least in the most essential parts, a data space governance framework should be created in parallel. The organisational form, type of governance authority and its role, and the governance framework may evolve over time due to legal requirements or business and technological developments.

#### Determining an organisational form and establishing a data space

A data space usually starts when several organisations have data sharing needs (a *data space initiative*). The members need to reach several basic agreements — that they want to work towards establishing a data space, what sector or sectors they want to target and how they would work together. These agreements do not need to be formalised. While developing the business model, they also consider a non-exhaustive list of questions decisive for the organisational form:

- Should the future data space be a permanent or temporary (even if multiyear) establishment?
- Should the future data space have a legal personality?
- Does the future data space aim to generate profits for its members (i.e. the legal entities that create it)?
- What country will be the primary place of establishment (i.e. headquarters) of the future data space?
- What level of involvement do the members of the data space initiative want to have in managing and operating the future data space?

**Data space without legal personality (unincorporated data space).** Various contractual arrangements can give structure to the data space as a multiyear project — joint ventures that are not separate legal forms, strategic alliance agreements, consortium agreements. For simplicity all such agreements are called *founding agreements* and the parties to them *data space members*. These founding agreements are governed by national civil law that may have specific requirements for their content and form (e.g. simple written form or notarised). Unincorporated joint ventures, consortia and similar cooperation are temporary arrangements created to achieve a certain goal; the founding agreement must therefore contain provisions on when the agreement is terminated. Due to the complexity of the arrangements, a written recording of any such agreements is strongly advisable, but no other actions (e.g. authentication by a notary or registration) are usually necessary.

*Consequences of this organisational option and contractual safeguards.* Unincorporated cooperations do not have their own resources and rely solely on the resources, capacities and capabilities of their members. It is advisable to expressly allocate in the contract the different roles and tasks to be performed by each member. Because data management of third parties is expected, the founding agreement must include clauses addressing data management: if a data space handles personal data, it is necessary to determine which member is a data processor as required by the GDPR; even if it does not, it is still necessary to determine which member(s) are responsible for data security and what their responsibilities are. It is advisable for members to design and sign a separate data management agreement. Because no legal person is created, legal liabilities and ownership remain by default with the individual members and must be specified in the founding agreements. Usually all results of the conducted work are owned by the party that generates them; members can agree on joint ownership, in which case they need to agree how they will dispose of joint property rights, including transferring, licencing and distributing the royalties. In relations to third parties, members are solely liable for their actions and no limitation by the founding contract is possible. An unincorporated entity must also decide who represents the data space towards other data space participants and third parties, because the unincorporated data space itself is not able to conclude any agreements in its name.

*Creation of Governance Authority (unincorporated).* Members can set up a governance authority for their cooperation according to their needs and wishes. There are no legal requirements for equal or any other specific representation in decision-making, which may lead to power imbalances between contracting parties; it would therefore be important to follow the best practices of corporate governance. The governance authority typically consists of all members of the consortium/joint venture and is ultimately responsible for decision-making. It can be called "general assembly", "steering group", or else. Its decisions are adopted unanimously and are binding on the members of the founding agreement. Any individuals active in the governance authority remain employees on the payroll of individual members and are subject to the direction and supervision of these members. An unincorporated data space is unlikely to be sustainable over time or to develop beyond a certain initial point; for this, a data space should become incorporated.

**Data space with legal personality (incorporated data space).** Currently existing data spaces that do not have profit-making as their purpose have frequently established themselves as **associations** and sometimes as **foundations**; a **limited liability company** is the most popular legal form for data spaces with profit-making objectives. A for-profit legal entity may acquire a special status of "benefit corporations" in some countries (e.g. Italy, France and Germany). Examples given: **Europeana**, the Common European Data Space for cultural heritage, founded as a foundation under Dutch law ("stichting"); **Catena X**, created as an association under German law ("eingetragener Verein"), a legal form not allowed to pursue commercial purposes; **Mobility Data Space** (aka DRM Datenraum Mobilität), a limited liability company under German law ("Gesellschaft mit beschränkter Haftung").

Legal forms created under EU law could be particularly attractive for data spaces targeting cross-border markets and for Common European Data Spaces:

- **European Digital Infrastructure Consortium** (a special legal form created for Common European Data Spaces and special multi-country projects);
- **European Company** (a for-profit legal entity, similar to the limited liability company);
- **European Cooperative Society** (a non-profit with characteristics of a cooperative and public limited company); and
- **European Economic Interest Grouping** (a non-profit legal entity similar to a partnership).

*European Digital Infrastructure Consortium.* Can be created only for the purpose of implementing multi-country projects. Must have at least three Member States as members; the characteristic of a member is that it provides financial (money) or non-financial (assets) contributions, and the consequence of membership is that a Member State has voting rights in the assembly of members. An EDIC is created via a decision of the European Commission: Member States submit an application including a technical description of the multi-country project, the proposed statutes, and a declaration from the host Member State on whether it recognises the EDIC as an international body and international organisation. Benefits: subject to virtually the same legal rules in all EU Member States; possible tax benefits if the host Member State recognises EDICs as international bodies or organisations; diverse membership including third states, international organisations, and private actors; potentially more effective investment vehicles. The European Commission will have a veto right over actions financed under centrally-managed Union programmes (such as the Digital Europe Programme, Horizon Europe and similar).

Once the members agree on the legal form, they need to sign a founding agreement (usually called a statute, articles of incorporation or articles of association). Its form and minimum contents are regulated by national or EU law; the main elements are name and address of the registered office, purpose and intended activities, method of convening of the general assembly and appointment and dismissal of directors, powers of representation to third parties, certain monetary obligations between the organisation and its members, admission of new members, and what happens to the organisation's property in the case of dissolution. Converting from a for-profit legal form to a non-profit is rather difficult due to the change in applicable taxation rules; a company created under the national law of one EU Member State cannot, in most cases, move its headquarters to another EU Member State without first dissolving and re-establishing itself.

*Consequences of this organisational option.* The data space becomes a legal person separate from its members: it can own property, enter into contractual relationships, be a beneficiary of legal rights, be responsible and liable for its own obligations and employ its own staff. Results of any work performed are its own (intellectual) property. Members have the right to participate in the main governance body and decide on the most important, strategic questions; control by members is rarely comprehensive. Third parties may participate as data providers, service providers, data recipients and have a contractual relationship with the data space, but they do not have decision rights.

*Creation of governance authority (incorporated).* Incorporated data spaces need bodies to create, maintain, and enforce their internal rules and manage day-to-day operations. For example, a non-profit in the legal form of an association must comply with a legal requirement to have two bodies: a **general assembly of members** (representative body, makes the most important decisions) and a **management board** (executive body, puts decisions into practice, appointed or elected by the general assembly and accountable to it). A data space's bodies jointly constitute a **data space governance authority**, though data spaces may choose a different name. Its form, composition and competencies are determined jointly by the founding members and are strongly impacted by the business model and legal form. If members want to involve third parties (stakeholders that are not data space members) in governance, they can foresee special bodies (e.g. advisory board, community board); such bodies cannot take binding decisions over a data space — only the assembly of the data space members can — but may provide opinions, views and observations. If a data space is created based on an already existing legal person that runs other business operations, it is advisable to create a separate body solely for managing the data space.

#### Data space governance framework

A governance framework sets out roles, responsibilities and procedures for effective and efficient operation; it consists of the organisation's internal objectives, values, policies, practices and accountabilities. It is influenced by and based on external legal and regulatory rules. Some legal rules are mandatory and must be reflected in the governance framework — for example, an organisation in the legal form of an association must have at least two bodies; a qualified trust service provider is subject to high security requirements and must have a special (cyber)security policy and guidelines on how to select trustworthy systems for data storage; an organisation processing personal data on a large scale must have a data protection officer as per the GDPR.

**Elements of a data space governance framework.** It is practical to distinguish at least two different elements: rules defining purely internal roles, responsibilities and procedures; and rules that also directly define the data space's relationships with third parties. Importantly, terms and conditions of use of a data space, and contracts between the data space and its service providers and other participants, are **not** part of the data space governance framework — they are operational and relate only to a concrete relationship, and are based on and result from the governance framework. Data product contracts and data services contracts are also **not** part of a data space governance framework, because they are contracts between third parties and do not involve a data space as a contract party at all.

*Internal rules of a data space.* Contained in the founding agreement and in data space bylaws. Bylaws contain procedures that keep the organisation running: the number of members constituting quorum for a general assembly meeting; how and when to gather an extraordinary general assembly meeting; details on voting procedures, including depending on the matter discussed; how to appoint and dissolve a committee or working group; rules on decision-making by the executive body; rules for approval of large contracts; and other procedures. Violation of these internal rules may invalidate legal relations with third parties (i.e. nullify contracts) and lead to civil law liability of a data space or its members. It is very important not to mix up the founding agreement with any other internal rules, because any change to the founding agreement must be reported to the registry of legal entities in the country where the data space is established.

*Rules that directly define relations with third parties.* Each data space can draw up various policies, statements, guidelines and related procedures. Some are required by law — an example of a must-have policy is the privacy and data protection policy, mandatory for any organisation processing or controlling personal data. Others are needs-based: a policy on technical specifications (standards, APIs, semantic models, provenance and traceability) used by a data space, or a cybersecurity policy defining internal measures and expectations towards data space service providers. A very large data space with many participants selling and buying data may consider adopting a competition policy and a dispute resolution policy and mechanism. A policy can also be adopted due to strategic objectives, for instance a sustainability policy or a stakeholder-engagement policy. Unless a policy is mandated by law, a data space can change or withdraw it completely without any legal consequences.

**Creation, maintenance and realisation of a data space governance framework.** All documents comprising the governance framework should be prepared and discussed by the executive body of the data space governance authority or by specially created working groups. The decision on adoption should be taken by the representative body of a data space. The general representative body can also adopt rules allowing the executive body to adopt certain policies or changes to policies — advisable for technical issues where quick decision-making is necessary. Once approved, the data space governance framework shall be documented; the artefact can be called any suitable name (currently the term "rulebook" is popular — the source cites the **IDSA Rule Book** and the **SITRA Rulebook**) and should be expressed in a human-readable and, if possible, machine-readable format. The framework must be kept up to date; monitoring of new developments lies with the executive body of the data space authority. Realisation of the governance framework is the responsibility of the executive body.

### Participation Management

> **Source** · DSSC Blueprint v3.0 › Business and Organisational Building Blocks › Governance Building Blocks › Participation Management

The Participation Management building block outlines governance processes for managing participant engagement in data spaces: identifying participants, setting rules for data transactions and service provision, onboarding, and offboarding. It provides guidelines for efficient and secure participation by integrating relationships with other governance aspects like regulatory compliance and identity management. It describes the operational processes, but some of them may need to be put into practice via the technical building blocks.

#### Scope and objectives

Data space participants are those committed to the governance framework of a particular data space and having a set of rights and obligations stemming from this framework. It concerns **all organizations and individuals** who join to engage in data transactions, facilitate transactions via service offerings, or act as data rights holders. Before formally joining, potential participants are referred to as **candidates**. Participation processes *within* the data space governance authority are out of scope — they are handled in *Organisational Form and Governance Authority*.

Participation Management is closely connected to other governance dimensions that ensure trust and compliance: Regulatory Compliance (adherence to GDPR and DGA); Contractual Frameworks (General Terms and Conditions and agreements that define rights, obligations, and exit procedures); Identity and Verification; Technical Interoperability (standards for APIs, data formats, and security protocols); Data Quality and Observability; and Trust Anchors and Conformity Schemes.

#### Roles and Responsibilities of Data Space Participants

Roles within a data space can vary depending on context — a participant may act as a provider of a data product in one transaction and as a consumer in another. Roles are outlined in the Data Space Rulebook, which serves as the documentation of the data space governance framework for operational use.

- **Data Providers** are organisations that supply data to the data space. They generate and share data sets that can be used by others.
- **Data Recipients** consume data from the data space for purposes such as analysis, decision-making or product development, and therefore directly engage in the process of data transaction. For both types of participants, participation management needs to ensure the management of permissions, support of data quality standards, and mechanisms to monitor and report data utilization.
- **Intermediaries and Operators** facilitate the exchange of data between providers and users. Even though they do not engage in data transactions, they are crucial participants, which is why participation management should facilitate them to ensure adherence to interoperability standards.
- **Data Rights Holders** are individuals or organizations that hold the rights to the data. They decide the terms and conditions under which their data can be shared and used within the data space.

The governance authority might formulate more specific roles per data space, documented in the rulebook. For some data spaces, distinguishing between participants who form the governance authority and those who do not might be needed — **data space members** can be introduced as an additional role for organisations that found the data space, sign the founding agreements and create the data space governance authority. **External stakeholders** are organizations not directly involved in the data space but significantly affected by or having impact on the data space ecosystem (for instance, farmers in an agricultural data space); this requires the governance authority to understand their concerns and needs and foster transparency.

#### Internal Governance Processes supporting Participation Management

To participate effectively and responsibly, organisations must establish internal governance processes that ensure clarity of roles, legal and technical accountability, and alignment with broader trust requirements. **ISO/IEC 38500 "Governance of IT for the organisation"** provides a relevant foundation for this work.

A key aspect of readiness is the internal definition and communication of roles related to data space engagement, including determining: who has authority to act on behalf of the organization; how decisions are taken regarding data access and sharing; and how responsibilities are distributed across teams managing legal compliance, data stewardship, and IT integration. Internal governance frameworks should also support the definition and enforcement of data rights, particularly where data rights holders and data providers are not the same entity. Governance processes must ensure that data quality is monitored and maintained throughout the data lifecycle. Observability is another essential governance function. Effective governance must also provide the ability to document and track data provenance and lineage. To remain effective over time, internal governance should include continuous review of participation-related processes.

> The source notes that this guidance reflects governance principles also outlined in the upcoming **CEN/CENELEC Workshop Agreement "Trusted Data Transaction – Part 2: Trustworthiness Requirements"**, which is under expert consultation and expected to be published soon.

Participation Management spans two layers:

- **Operational Processes** (human-driven governance activities): defining roles and responsibilities (data providers, recipients, data intermediaries, data rights holders); onboarding and offboarding workflows, including eligibility checks and contractual acceptance; decision-making on compliance, risk management, and participant obligations; continuous review and adaptation of governance frameworks.
- **Technical Processes** (implemented via building blocks and automated systems): registries maintaining authoritative participant lists and trust anchors; identification and attestation systems for credential issuance, verification, and lifecycle management; onboarding tooling for digital acceptance of terms, automated compliance checks, and secure credential provisioning; monitoring and audit services for observability of transactions, anomaly detection, and traceability.

#### Onboarding

The data space governance authority defines the minimum requirements for participation, which are outlined in the **General Terms and Conditions**. These cover the rules for joining, including identity verification, compliance checks, attestations (first-, second-, or third-party), technical standards (e.g. APIs, data formats, languages), and baseline security requirements. They also clarify roles, responsibilities, and obligations for all participants.

To join, a candidate submits an application that specifies their intended role and use of the data space. The Governance Authority reviews the application against the established criteria and decides whether to grant or deny access. Alternatively, the authority may allow direct participation for any candidate that accepts the General Terms and Conditions and fulfils the admission criteria, without requiring a formal application process. In both approaches, participants must be notified of the decision, and in cases of denial, reasons should be communicated. Once approved, participants formally accept the General Terms and Conditions, either by signing an agreement or through digital confirmation; the method may depend on the candidate's role. Upon acceptance, membership credentials are issued to enable interaction with the data space and other participants.

The onboarding process is supported by the **Data Space Registry**, which provides visibility into data space rules, trust anchors, and conformity schemes used to verify compliance. The **Rulebook** complements the General Terms and Conditions by describing technical and operational standards in more detail. Ongoing monitoring is essential to ensure that participants continue to comply. Rules for participation may vary depending on the type of data being exchanged and the applicable legal framework — for example, personal data must be handled in accordance with GDPR.

**Conditions to join a data space.** Admission policies within the General Terms and Conditions describe the conditions and eligibility criteria that third parties need to meet: identity verification, compliance check, technical requirements, access control setup, data quality standards or security policies. The governance authority must balance lowering barriers to entry (flexible rules) and promoting interoperability and data quality (strict rules). The General Terms and Conditions should include requirements for **verifying participants** (e.g. strong identification) and for setting requirements for the products and services available in the data space (e.g. language, data formats). Depending on the Governance Framework, the Data Space Governance Authority must provide mechanisms to check if all admission/eligibility criteria are fulfilled by the candidate. Both the application route and the direct-acceptance route require constant monitoring that all participants act in accordance with the data space's rules and obligations.

#### Participation management in operational and scaling phase

Technical onboarding by the governance authority is a process to enable new participants a seamless connection and instant readiness to actively engage; this can be aided with initial data integration support to ensure data or services meet quality format and standards. Active monitoring extends beyond initial onboarding. Feedback from participants is crucial, enabling the governance authority to make data-driven adjustments to onboarding procedures. As the data space grows, the governance authority needs to regularly screen the governance framework and eventually adapt it to address emerging needs and challenges — regulatory changes, or expansion to new industries, companies, or countries with distinct regulations and standards. Potential adaptations must remain in line with the data space's central mission/vision.

#### Offboarding

**Reasons for offboarding.** The regular case is a participant simply wanting to exit. A different reason is forced exit, caused e.g. by the participant not complying with the data space's rules; in such a case the Data Space Governance Authority needs to formulate rules and processes of how to enforce an exit. Another scenario is exception handling, when a participating company goes bankrupt and cannot fulfil its obligations anymore; in such a case the Data Space Governance Authority needs to inform all affected parties and enforce the exit.

**Offboarding process.** The governance framework, especially its Terms and Conditions, guides the exit process. Exiting the data space requires proof that all contracts made with other participants have been fulfilled and no contractual obligations remain open. The participant informs the governance authority about the desired exit — digitally or in written form, as stated in the Terms and Conditions. After checking whether all offboarding criteria are met, the governance authority confirms the exit to the participant. Criteria and elements that ensure careful and efficient offboarding are:

- **Documentation of exit procedures** — a documented offboarding procedure including detailed steps for data transfer, access termination, and contract closure; this entails for instance continuous life cycle management of credentials.
- **Data Transfer and Deletion Protocols** — clear protocols for the secure transfer or deletion of data, in accordance with the corresponding licenses agreed upon, the data space policies and any applicable legal requirements.
- **Notification** — timely and clear notifications to all relevant parties about the participant's exit, including details about data transfer, access termination, and any remaining obligations.
- **Verification of compliance** — a thorough review to verify that all contractual and compliance obligations have been met before finalizing, including that any financial or legal obligations are settled.
- **Offboarding support** — support during the transition period to address issues or questions, including assistance with data transfer.
- **Periodic Framework Reviews** — periodic and thorough reviews of the governance framework, incorporating necessary updates in response to legislative changes.

> *The Catena-X seven-step onboarding process (Initial Contact & Opportunity Assessment; Business Value Definition; Organizational & Technical Preparation; Registration & Identity Verification; Connector and Service Configuration; Integration, Testing & Conformance; Go-Live & Ongoing Operation), drawn from the Catena-X Onboarding Guide (v1.1), is presented upstream as a best-practice example. ### Regulatory Compliance

> **Source** · DSSC Blueprint v3.0 › Business and Organisational Building Blocks › Legal Building Blocks › Regulatory Compliance

This building block guides the data space governance authority in applying legal rules to a data space's design and operation: defining participant roles and responsibilities, establishing internal policies, and continuously monitoring regulatory compliance. It also assists participants in understanding their rights and obligations under regulatory frameworks relevant to their role or to a specific data transaction. Regulatory compliance is an ongoing practice throughout the data space lifecycle.

#### Scope and objectives

Key elements of this building block:

- **Triggers** — Elements, criteria or events (e.g. data type, nature of participant or domain) that have occurred in a particular context of a data space and signal that a specific legal framework must or should be applied.
- **Data space requirements** — Regulatory provisions that explicitly refer to data spaces.
- **Additional legal considerations** — Other important legal considerations, e.g. cybersecurity law.
- **LegalTech and RegTech** — Technical tools or techniques designed to address certain legal requirements (such as smart contracts, secure processing environment, privacy-enhancing technologies etc.).
- **Regulatory Compliance Flowcharts** — Step-by-step guidance helping to assess the applicability of a specific legal framework, operationalizing the triggers.

The main objectives are: guiding data space initiatives on organising compliance with relevant legislation throughout the lifecycle of data space development; supporting governance authorities in identifying relevant legal issues that may warrant strategies, procedures and mechanisms; helping identify legal entitlements to data and the rights and obligations of participants in relation to them; and providing an overview of additional resources to help assess obligations under EU law.

The source states it falls outside the remit of the blueprint to provide an exhaustive listing of legal frameworks. It is up to the sectoral data spaces to do a further mapping and identify additional legal requirements from sectoral legislation, and relevant national legislation must be analysed accordingly. An exception is made for the **European Health Data Space Regulation (EHDSR)** due to its impact on interoperability with other Common European Data Spaces.

> On the **ePrivacy Directive**: while it aims to harmonise national provisions on the protection of fundamental rights and freedoms in the electronic communication sector, it may also apply to non-personal data. Per **EDPB Guidelines 2/2023**, this is especially the case for **art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive**, imposing on Member States the obligation to ensure that "the storing of information, or the gaining of access to information already stored, in the terminal equipment of a subscriber or user" is only allowed on the basis of consent or necessity for specific purposes set out in that Article. Following the CJEU **Planet 49** judgment, "the protection applies to any information stored in such terminal equipment, regardless of whether or not it is personal data".

#### The concept of triggers within a data space

An element, criterion or event that has occurred in a particular context of a data space is referred to as a **'trigger'**. It signals that a particular legal framework must or should be applied. Triggers may be classified into:

- **Types of data** — Depending on the precise kinds of data, it may trigger the need to comply with, for example, the GDPR or Data Act requirements.
- **Types of data space participants** — Depending on legal status, such as being a public or specific private body, different regulations may apply. For example, the Data Governance Act regulates the operation of data intermediation services providers or data altruism organisations.
- **Types of use cases** — Domains and/or use cases, such as health, mobility, etc., where domain-specific regulations would apply.

The criterion for selecting the priority triggers for this version of the Blueprint "was based on the necessity to address specific aspects of the operation of these elements within the governance or contractual framework of a data space."

**Types of data.** While the current classification of data types within the EU digital regulatory framework lacks methodological consistency, a key distinction is usually made between personal and non-personal data, though this distinction is imperfect. Certain categories of data may be subject to particular **"protection"** regimes:

- *Personal data protection* — According to the GDPR, the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union and the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU), the protection of natural persons regarding the processing of personal data is a fundamental right. Special categories of data enjoy specific protection under the GDPR.
- *Intellectual property and trade secrets* — Primarily intellectual property rights (namely copyright), sui generis database rights, or rights granted to trade secret holders. The classification of datasets as personal or non-personal, or the nature of the ownership of (private/public) datasets, does not impact the potential application of other protection regimes. Where a dataset containing personal data is kept as a trade secret or enjoys copyright and/or sui generis database protection, the data protection principles and data subjects' rights provided by the GDPR still apply.

The **"access"** regime largely depends on the nature of a data holder:

- *Privately-held data* — Governed mainly by the **Data Act**, which imposes on certain entities the obligation to make product and related services data (IoT data) accessible to users (**Article 3 of the Data Act**) or to provide data based on an exceptional need expressed by a public sector body (B2G) (**Article 14 of the Data Act**).
- *Data held by public sector bodies (PSB)* — Distinguished by their level of openness. **High-value datasets** are of particular importance for society, the environment and the economy, and can be re-usable for any purpose. The **Data Governance Act (DGA)** outlines the conditions under which public sector bodies can allow the reuse of certain protected data (for example, data protected on grounds of commercial confidentiality, including business, professional and company secrets).

**Types of participants.** Some participants are entities whose existence or specific functions have been regulated by law. Participants fall into two main groups, private and public entities, but in the latest EU data regulatory frameworks the distinction is becoming blurred. The source presents participant types on a spectrum, from those most likely private (such as gatekeepers), through borderline or uncertain cases (such as data altruism organisations), to public entities (such as public sector bodies).

**Sector-specific use cases.** Specific sectoral regulations may also impact the development of a particular use case. In some situations it will be important to analyse both the sectoral regulations and the broader, horizontal framework.

#### Data space requirements

**Data Act.** A key piece of legislation that directly regulates data spaces. It requires data space participants who offer data or data services to meet essential requirements to ensure data interoperability, effective data-sharing mechanisms, and the proper functioning of Common European Data Spaces. The obligations of **Article 33 of the Data Act** should, by their very nature, be considered intrinsically linked to the infrastructure and governance framework of the data space; the essential requirements to facilitate interoperability should be addressed by the data space governance authority at the level of the data space, taking into account available (harmonised) standards and any potential further delegated acts or guidance adopted by the European Commission. On the basis of **art. 3(4) Data Act**, the Commission requested relevant European standardisation organisations to draft harmonised standards satisfying the essential requirements laid down in paragraph 1 of that Article.

**European Health Data Space Regulation.** Currently the only legal instrument setting in place a common European data space. According to **art. 52(12) EHDSr**, Member States and the Commission shall seek to ensure interoperability of **HealthData@EU** with other relevant Common European Data Spaces as referred to in the Data Governance Act and Data Act. In addition, by implementing acts, the European Commission may set out common specifications for the interoperability and architecture of HealthData@EU with other relevant Common European Data Spaces.

> **Ambiguous:** the source numbers both the *Data Act* and the *European Health Data Space Regulation* sub-sections `3.2.1`. The duplicate numbering is upstream's.

#### Additional legal considerations — cybersecurity law

The **NIS-2 Directive** lays down risk management measures and reporting obligations building resilience and incident response capacities across the Union. It applies to medium and large entities, as well as to an entity of any size that:

- Is a public administration entity,
- Is the sole provider of a service in a member state,
- Could have an impact on public safety, security or health if its services are disrupted,
- Could induce systemic risks or have cross-border impacts if its services are disrupted,
- Is a critical entity because of its importance at the regional or national level for its sector or type of service,
- Provides services by public electronic communications networks, publicly available electronic communications services, trust service providers, or top-level domain name registries and domain name system service providers.

Entities of any size that meet any of the first five criteria, and medium-size entities that meet the sixth criterion, are considered **essential entities** (e.g. energy, transport, banking, financial market infrastructure, health, drinking water, wastewater, digital infrastructure, public administration and space). **Important entities** are covered organisations in, for example, waste management, chemical production and distribution, manufacturers of medical devices, electrical equipment, transport equipment, and digital providers of online marketplaces, search engines and social networking platforms.

Entities can prepare for the requirements laid down in **art. 21(2) NIS-2**, with the minimum catalogue of risk-management measures:

- policies on risk analysis and information system security;
- incident handling;
- business continuity, such as backup management and disaster recovery, and crisis management;
- supply chain security;
- security in network and information systems acquisition, development and maintenance, including vulnerability handling and disclosure;
- policies and procedures to assess the effectiveness of cybersecurity risk-management measures;
- basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training;
- policies and procedures regarding the use of cryptography and, where appropriate, encryption;
- human resources security, access control policies and asset management;
- the use of multi-factor authentication or continuous authentication solutions, secured voice, video and text communications and secured emergency communication systems within the entity, where appropriate.

The source raises, without resolving, whether data spaces as a whole can be considered a critical infrastructure in one of the mentioned sectors, and whether a data space can be considered a "provider of online marketplace" under **Annex II of NIS-2** — noting that the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive defines "online marketplace" as a service using software, including a website, part of a website or an application, operated by or on behalf of a trader which allows consumers to conclude distance contracts with other traders or consumers. It concludes that "such consideration remains open to explore."

#### Legal Tech and RegTech in the context of data spaces

**Legal Tech** has no settled legal definition but broadly refers to the use of technology to deliver or support legal services, in three stages of development: *Legal Tech 1.0*, tools that assist legal professionals with routine tasks; *Legal Tech 2.0*, systems that automate specific legal processes; *Legal Tech 3.0*, technologies that employ artificial intelligence to perform autonomous decision-making. For the purposes of data spaces, **Legal Tech 2.0 is the most relevant**.

Categories of Legal Tech per functionality: **Automated Contract Management Systems** (translating contractual terms into machine-readable workflows); **Smart Contracts** (self-executing and enforcing terms when preset conditions are met, built on DLT — their immutability requires governance to ensure reversibility and alignment with EU obligations); **Compliance Automation Platforms** (automating GDPR-related workflows such as privacy-by-design, impact assessments, and record-keeping).

**RegTech** ("regulatory technology") refers to the use of digital technologies, especially data analytics and automation, to make regulatory monitoring, reporting, and compliance more effective and efficient. Typical tools include eKYC/identity verification and screening, transaction monitoring, regulatory reporting & filings, policy and obligation management, and audit capabilities.

#### Types of data (Data as a Trigger)

> **Source** · DSSC Blueprint v3.0 › Business and Organisational Building Blocks › Legal Building Blocks › Regulatory Compliance › Types of data (Data as a Trigger)

> **Ambiguous:** the page opens by stating that the overview "aims to provide an exhaustive taxonomy of data types within the applicable EU regulatory frameworks" while in the same sentence saying the classification "remains a work in progress and may be adjusted or further developed in the next version of the Blueprint". The two claims are in tension in the source.

The page lists types of data that may trigger the need for compliance with various legal frameworks, with a description and assessment of the impact.

**Personal data.** The data protection legal framework — including the **GDPR**, as well as the **Law Enforcement Directive** and the **e-Privacy Directive** (if particular circumstances apply) — remains fully applicable to the processing of personal data within the context of a data space. Neither the involvement of a data space, nor that of a personal data intermediary, relinquishes the parties involved in such processing of their duties as data controllers or processors. The clear establishment of the roles of data controller and data (sub-)processor should be a priority, taking into account the essential criterion of decision-making powers regarding purpose and means of processing. Issues of data protection should be an important consideration from the very start of the design of a data space and throughout all its development stages. The GDPR also applies to **mixed datasets** (comprised of both non-personal and personal data), and this remains valid even if personal data represents only a small part of the dataset. The concept of "purpose" under data protection law should be given particular attention.

The Spanish Data Protection Authority (AEPD), referencing the **EDPB-EDPS Joint Opinion 03/2022** on the Proposal for a Regulation on the European Health Data Space, highlights the importance of a data protection policy stating "how the principles and rights set out in the data protection regulation and the guidelines in this document are to be implemented in a concrete, practical and effective manner." Elements it lists include the involvement of data protection officers (DPOs) and advisors in the design of data spaces; procedures for authorising the processing of personal data within the data space; a precise definition of the purposes of data processing; and risk management, including data protection impact assessments (DPIAs) coordinated between involved parties.

*Synthetic data* can be understood as data artificially generated from original data, preserving the statistical properties of said original data; some data may also be completely artificially created without an underlying real-world data asset. Per the AEPD, the use of synthetic data, along with techniques such as generalisation, suppression, or the use of Secure Processing Environments, can be a way to comply with data minimisation or privacy by design/default principles. When personal data is used to generate synthetic data, that is part of a processing operation and therefore subject to the GDPR; however, depending on the original data, the model and additional techniques applied, the synthetic data can be anonymous data.

*Traceability.* Data traceability should help to identify roles and implement access control and access logging policies, addressing GDPR transparency requirements, enabling the effective exercise of data subjects' rights such as consent management, facilitating controller obligations, and allowing Supervisory Authorities to exercise their powers in accordance with **Article 58(1) of the GDPR**. Keeping a log of accesses and participants' actions could be a way to implement the obligations laid down by **art. 32(1) GDPR**, requiring controller and processor to implement appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk.

*Technical implementation.* A relevant solution could be to implement the **W3C's Data Privacy Vocabulary**, which enables the expression of machine-readable metadata about the use and processing of personal data based on legislative requirements such as the GDPR.

*Special categories of personal data ("sensitive" data).* According to **art. 9(1) GDPR**, personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade-union membership, genetic data, biometric data processed solely to identify a human being, health-related data, data concerning a person's sex life or sexual orientation, shall be considered special categories of personal data, the processing of which is generally prohibited unless one of the strictly enumerated legal bases set out in paragraph (2) occurs. They shall be strictly interpreted and considered separately for each processing activity. Processing special categories of data referred to in **Article 9(2)(g)** (essential public interest), **(h)** (purposes of preventive or occupational medicine, assessment of the worker's capacity to work, medical diagnosis, provision of health or social care or treatment, or management of health and social care systems and services) and **(i)** (public interest in the field of public health) of the GDPR has to provide appropriate safeguards and has to be covered by a regulation having the force of law. The GDPR also requires assessing: the necessity of processing the data (including appropriateness); the proportionality of the processing; and whether the processing activity can be considered "high-risk processing" — if so, there must be an appropriate data protection impact assessment that manages the high risk and demonstrates passing the assessment of necessity, appropriateness and strict proportionality.

*Biometric data* is defined in **Art. 4(14) GDPR** as personal data resulting from specific technical processing relating to the physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics of a natural person, which allow or confirm the unique identification of that natural person, such as facial images or dactyloscopic data. As one of the special categories, its processing is prohibited in principle. In accordance with the **AI Act**, AI systems used for biometric categorisation based on sensitive attributes (protected under Article 9(1) GDPR) and emotion recognition should be classified as high-risk, in so far as these are not prohibited under that regulation. Biometric systems intended to be used solely for the purpose of enabling cybersecurity and personal data protection measures should not be considered high-risk AI systems.

**Intellectual Property Rights / Trade Secret-Protected Data(sets).** IP law can confer rights over datasets, often through copyright and the sui generis database right; data may also be protected by trade secrets, a separate legal regime. It is the responsibility of each data space participant to ensure legal compliance and to have an authorisation and/or legal basis for data sharing if data is protected by copyright, sui generis or trade secrets.

- *Copyright law* protects creative works like text, images, video, and sound, and also software (e.g. source code) and databases. Copyright does not protect data itself. To be protected by copyright, a database has to be original, reflect the author's intellectual creation, and be fixed in tangible form; a specific test of originality applies (whether the selection or arrangement of contents constitutes the author's own intellectual creation). Purely factual information is usually not eligible for protection (**Article 2, InfoSoc Directive**).
- *Sui Generis Database Right* (**EU Database Directive 96/9/EC**) — grants rights to creators who made a qualitatively and/or quantitatively substantial investment in obtaining, verifying, or presenting contents; provides the right to prevent unauthorized extraction or re-utilisation; defines databases as collections arranged systematically and individually accessible.
- *Trade Secret Protection* (**EU Trade Secrets Directive 2016/943**) — encompasses various data types, requiring secrecy and commercial value; enforceable rights against unlawful use and misappropriation without conferring property rights; secrecy is preserved as long as persons having access to information are bound by confidentiality agreements.

Solutions at the data space level: the acknowledgement of intellectual property and quasi-IP rights (trade secrets) should be addressed in the intellectual property policy within the general terms & conditions and in the intellectual property clauses of particular data product contracts. Data holders are responsible for providing information about the IP rights and/or trade secrets they possess over particular datasets before sharing them with potential data recipients. Specific legal provisions concerning trade secrets in the context of data sharing can be found in the **Data Act**. Examples of legal, organisational and technical measures to preserve IP rights or trade secrets can be found in the **European Health Data Space Regulation** — data access contractual arrangements, specific obligations in relation to the rights granted to the data recipient, pre-processing the data to generate derived data that protects a trade secret but still has utility for the user, or configuration of the secure processing environment so that such data is not accessible by the data recipient (**recital 60, art. 53 EHDS-R**).

**Non-personal data.** *The source's table row for this data type has no description or impact assessment — the cell is empty.*

**IoT Data.** The **Data Act** lays down a harmonised framework specifying the rules for using product data or related service data, including data from Internet of Things devices, smartphones, and cars. It imposes the obligation on data holders to make data available to users and third parties of the user's choice in certain circumstances, and ensures that data holders make data available to data recipients in the Union under fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms and conditions and in a transparent manner. These provisions apply irrespective of the personal/non-personal character of the data; where processing of personal data is involved, the GDPR still applies. Data spaces should pay attention to **Chapter II of the Data Act**, especially for data transactions involving product data and related service data, and should consider the rights of users of connected products or related services towards the data they produce — including access to and use of the data for any lawful purpose, with some exceptions. The data provided to the user should be of the same quality as the data available to the data holder and should be provided easily, securely, free of charge, and in a comprehensive, structured, commonly used and machine-readable format. Where the data transaction is concluded without the data user directly involved, the scope of such transactions is predefined by the contract with the user: data holders shall not make available non-personal product data to third parties for commercial or non-commercial purposes other than the fulfilment of such a contract. Data holders can also make their data available via third parties of their choice (for example, data intermediation service providers as defined by the DGA) for commercial purposes; these third parties then hold certain obligations towards the data they receive on behalf of the user, including being able to transfer the data access rights granted by the user to other third parties, including in exchange for compensation.

**High-Value Datasets (HVDs).** Defined in the **Open Data Directive** as "documents held by a public sector body, the reuse of which is associated with important benefits for society, the environment and the economy". HVDs can be re-usable for any purpose. Public sector bodies are not allowed to charge fees for the reuse of HVDs. The reuse of documents should not be subject to conditions, but some cases are justified by a public interest objective; in these situations public sector bodies might issue a license imposing conditions on the reuse dealing with issues such as liability, the protection of personal data, the proper use of documents, guaranteeing non-alteration and the acknowledgement of source.

The source closes by noting that there are categories of data for which the legal status is not known and which are therefore de facto under the control of the data holder.

#### Types of Participants (Participant as a Trigger)

> **Source** · DSSC Blueprint v3.0 › Business and Organisational Building Blocks › Legal Building Blocks › Regulatory Compliance › Types of Participants (Participant as a Trigger)

**Gatekeepers.** The **Digital Markets Act** defines gatekeepers as undertakings providing so-called "core platform services", such as online search engines, social networking services, video-sharing platform services, number-independent interpersonal communications services, operating systems, web browsers, etc. According to **Art. 3(1) DMA**, for an undertaking to be designated by the European Commission as a "gatekeeper", it has to have a significant impact on the internal market; provide a core platform service which is an important gateway for business users to reach end users; and enjoy an entrenched and durable position in its operations, or it is foreseeable that it will enjoy such a position in the near future. It also has to meet the requirements regarding an annual turnover above the threshold determined by the regulation. So far, the European Commission has designated the following gatekeepers: **Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Booking, ByteDance, Meta, and Microsoft**. This position is determined in relation to a specific core platform service. While the DMA does not aim to establish a framework for data sharing, it challenges the "data monopoly" of gatekeepers. Specific data-related obligations that may be relevant in the context of a data space:

- **Ban on data combination** — for example, combining personal data from one core platform service with personal data from any further core platform services, or from any other services provided by the gatekeeper or with personal data from third-party services (**art. 5(2)(b) DMA**);
- **Data silos** — prohibition to use, in competition with business users, not publicly available data generated or provided by those business users in the context of their use of the relevant core platform services (**art. 6(2) DMA**);
- **Data portability** — obligation to provide end users and third parties authorised by an end user with effective portability of data provided by the end user or generated through the activity of the end user in the context of the use of the relevant core platform service (**Article 6(9) DMA**);
- **Access to data generated by users** — obligation to provide business users and third parties authorised by a business user access and use of aggregated/non-aggregated data, including personal data, that is provided for or generated in the context of the use of the relevant core platform services (**Article 6(10) DMA**);
- **Access search data for online search engines** — providing online search engines fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms to ranking, query, click and view data in relation to free and paid search generated by end users on its online search engines (**Article 6(11) DMA**).

**Data Intermediation Service Providers (DISPs).** The **Data Governance Act (DGA)** sets out specific requirements for providing data intermediation services. Certain service functions in data spaces are likely to qualify as data intermediation services. A data space governance authority should evaluate to what extent it organises any services that may qualify as data intermediation services under the DGA; if this is the case, it will need to ensure compliance with the provisions of the DGA. Under the DGA, a "data intermediation service" (DIS) is defined as a service aiming to establish commercial relationships for the purposes of data sharing between an undetermined number of data subjects and data holders on the one hand and data users on the other. Providers intending to provide data intermediation services are required under the DGA to submit a notification to the competent national authority. The provision of data intermediation services is subject to a range of conditions, including a limitation on the use by the provider of the data for which it provides data intermediation services. The European Commission hosts a register of data intermediation services recognised in the European Union.

The term "data space intermediary" refers to "a data space participant that provides one or more enabling services while not directly participating in the data transactions"; "enabling services" refers to "a service that implements a data space functionality that enables data transactions for the transaction participants and/or operational processes for the governance authority." Not all data space intermediaries would be subject to the provisions of the DGA by default: some enabling services may not be aimed at establishing commercial relationships for the purposes of data sharing, and the services may not result in commercial relationships between an *undetermined* number of data subjects and data holders on the one hand and data users on the other.

Where data intermediation services relate to personal data, it is important to appropriately consider the different roles and responsibilities under the DGA and the GDPR. The source notes that for a transaction facilitated by a provider of data intermediation services it is difficult to establish who is acting as the controller, whether there are joint controllers, whether there is a processor and whether data users are data recipients; it may be important to clarify the respective responsibilities of particular participants by offering guidance.

**Data Altruism Organisations (DAOs).** In the context of data spaces, DAOs can take on a variety of roles as data space participants — data providers, transaction participants, and data space intermediaries (e.g. personal data intermediaries). It is important to address their participation in the data space, especially regarding value distribution aspects and their potential sponsoring by the data space.

**Researchers.** Researchers may join data spaces to make better use of their rights under legal frameworks; in the data-sharing context they can be both data recipients and data providers. Frameworks that facilitate access to data include provisions in the **Open Data Directive** promoting the re-use of public sector information; research exceptions in intellectual property law such as the text and data mining exception of **Art. 3 Copyright in the Digital Single Market Directive**; and **Art. 40 of the Digital Services Act** (under specific circumstances mentioned in **Art. 8 DSA**, and for purposes including detection, identification and understanding of systemic risks and assessment of their risk mitigation). Initiatives such as **Horizon Europe** further support open science and data sharing.

**Public Sector Bodies.** Public sector bodies perform legally defined duties for the benefit of the public interest. They can be subject to specific obligations to share certain categories of data, not only with data space participants but with other potential users outside of the data space as well. The data held by public sector bodies can be of structural relevance for a data space ecosystem.

### Contractual Framework

> **Source** · DSSC Blueprint v3.0 › Business and Organisational Building Blocks › Legal Building Blocks › Contractual Framework

The Contractual Framework building block describes the legally enforceable agreements that underlie the operation of a data space, as entered into by different parties in a relationship with the data space. Agreements are classified into three categories based on the nature of their subject matter: **Institutional agreements**, **Data-sharing agreements**, and **Services agreements**. These types differ with respect to the parties involved, their function, and the elements covered.

#### Scope and objectives

The building block supports data space participants and governing authorities by translating legally relevant elements (e.g. responsibilities, liability, rules on onboarding and offboarding, and commitments related to data transactions) into clear, legally valid, and enforceable agreements. It promotes awareness about the need to enable interoperable, automated, and scalable agreements, ensuring consistency across different levels (ecosystem, data space, use case, participant). It categorises and outlines the main agreements, and it identifies mandatory/non-negotiable elements of the contractual framework alongside negotiable terms.

The term 'agreement' is understood as the document, whether in physical or digital form, serving as evidence of a contract: a legally binding agreement between two or more parties with a shared interest, establishing mutual obligations enforceable by law. The current division is based on the **SITRA Rulebook model for a fair data economy**, with differences reflecting the different scope of the document.

#### Overview of the Contractual Framework

The Contractual Framework encompasses all agreements that govern the operation and activities of a data space and its participants. It defines the relationships, contractual rights, and obligations within the data space, implementing its governance framework, and renders part of its rules legally enforceable among participants, members, and enabling service providers. Beyond governance, it empowers participants — particularly data providers and, indirectly, data rights holders — to exercise their data sovereignty.

- **Institutional Agreements** — Define the foundational governance and common rules binding all data space participants. They provide the foundation for the creation of data spaces and establish a minimum mandatory governance framework at the data space level applicable to all participants. At a high level they serve as a common set of rules; at a more granular level they mandate specific terms & conditions to be included in data product contracts, for example by defining a limited number of predefined and standardised contractual clauses and/or licenses to be used by data providers. They may limit a participant's (particularly the data provider's) freedom to enter into data-sharing agreements, with the aim of reducing transaction costs and complexity while enhancing legal interoperability.
- **Data-Sharing Agreements** — Regulate rights and obligations between parties exchanging or accessing data. Data sharing is used as a general term referring to a heterogeneous range of actions: **Supply of data** (the generic notion of making data available to another party); **Transfer of data** (a specific form of supply where control over the data is passed to the recipient); **Porting of data** (a specific form of supply initiated by the data subject or controller, whereby data controlled by another party is transferred to themselves or to a designated third party); and **Remote or decentralized access and processing** (arrangements where data remain under the control of the provider, but authorized users can access, query, or process the data without obtaining a copy). Definitions of supply, transfer and porting are taken from the **ALI-ELI Principles for a Data Economy**. Data-sharing agreements are not binding on all data space participants; they only bind data transaction participants.
- **Services Agreements** — Set terms for providing and using data-related or enabling services within the data space. They divide into agreements for services related to data (e.g. Data Exchange & Interoperability Services) and agreements related to enabling services (e.g. Identity & Access Management Agreements). Services related to data include data processing, data trust, data escrow, and data intermediation services (known in the ALI-ELI Principles as data marketplace contracts): data trust contracts impose fiduciary duties on trustees to act for the benefit of entrusters or beneficiaries (**Principle 13**), data escrow contracts restrict the powers of data holders via a trusted third party to ensure regulatory compliance (**Principle 14**), and data marketplace contracts ensure intermediaries facilitate transactions without using the data for their own purposes (**Principle 15**). Data intermediation services are defined in **Art. 2(11) DGA** as a "service which aims to establish commercial relationships for the purposes of sharing data between an undetermined number of data subjects and data holders on the one hand and data users on the other, through technical, legal or other means, including for the purpose of exercising the rights of data subjects in relation to personal data".

**Mandatory vs Non-Mandatory agreements.** This is not a rigid binary distinction but a spectrum. Establishing the contractual framework first requires an assessment of the regulatory framework — the mandatory obligations imposed on data spaces as a whole or on data space participants — to determine which rules need to be complied with and addressed. Once this process is completed, the partners initiating the data space are free to define the remaining clauses according to the governance framework they wish to establish. Non-mandatory rules can only be valid and effective as long as they are consistent with the regulatory framework.

#### Functional descriptions of the contractual framework

**Founding agreement.** Establishes the data space and its governance authority, entered into by the founding members of the data space initiative. It sets out the purpose for which the data space is created. Different legal instruments can be used depending on the organisational form: **Articles of Association or Statutes**, used to establish the data space as a separate legal entity (the statute may allow onboarding of new members by allowing external parties to sign it); or **consortium agreements, joint ventures, strategic cooperation, and other cooperation agreements**, where the data space is not incorporated. The agreement-specific clauses may include: background and purpose (why the data space is created); identification of the parties (members of the data space initiative); general responsibilities and liability (in some cases — e.g. in joint ventures); reference to the chosen governance models; and the admission policy for data space members. An example of an artefact used to signal the intention to join a data space as a member is the **Accession agreement** (SITRA Rulebook).

**General Terms and Conditions.** An agreement providing legal enforceability to various elements of the governance framework, binding on all data space participants; in some instances they can be included in the founding agreement or directly referenced by it. They create and define the roles and responsibilities of the data space participants, including the data space governance authority, data provider, data recipient, and service providers. When a data space is established as a legal entity, the agreement may cover both the liability between participants and the liability between the participant and the legal entity. The areas regulated at the **data space level** include:

- **Admission policy for data space participants** — whether new participants are accepted, the conditions and eligibility criteria to join, the conditions to remain a participant, and the procedures for the removal of an existing participant. Participants join a data space by accepting the terms and conditions.
- **Intellectual property policy** — at a minimum, this specifies that none of these agreements should be construed as an assignment of IP rights; instead, non-exclusive licenses are granted. If new IP rights may emerge from collaboration (e.g. sui generis rights for databases), clauses to regulate ownership of IP rights could be included.
- **Data protection policy** — describes the data protection policy at data space level, referring to the obligations of the data space as a controller when processing the data of the participants, and may include references to the potential role of data spaces as processors. The data space may provide a technical framework that ensures the processing operations comply with GDPR by design; adherence to this framework can additionally be made legally binding. Templates for Data Processing agreements or Data Exchange agreements (e.g. iSHARE) can also be provided.
- **Technical standards** — outline and/or enforce specific technical standards for participants. The general terms and conditions may also restrict the data models and formats to be used in the data space.
- **Cybersecurity and risk management policies** — rules and procedures for the protection of technology and information assets, along with other identified risks to the data space infrastructure or participants.
- **Complaints policy and dispute resolution rules** — rules and procedures for filing a complaint, and for resolving disputes between participants, including with the data space governing authority.

Agreement-specific clauses related to the **data space level**: definitions (defining the roles of data space participants as well as potential third parties); role-specific conditions (rights and obligations corresponding to the role); responsibilities; fees and costs; confidentiality; IP rights; data protection; accession policy; technical standards and commitments; cybersecurity and risk management policies; complaints policy and dispute resolution rules.

Agreement-specific clauses related to the **transaction level**: general conditions for data sharing; **standardised licences model for data usage rights** — the general terms and conditions may limit the choice of licence or offer data providers a limited set of standardised licences, examples of which include *No limitations*; *Resharing with data space participants/internal use only*; *Non-commercial use only*; *Data enrichment before resharing*; *Data can be enriched with the data recipient's own data*; *Data can be enriched with data from others*; *Data can be enriched with the data recipient's own data before resharing on a non-commercial basis*; *Data can be enriched with data of others before resharing on a non-commercial basis*; *Ad hoc-licenses (as determined by the parties)*; **standardised terms and conditions for data products**; and **mechanisms to calculate fees** (if applicable).

**Agreements related to enabling services.** The Blueprint adopts a technical understanding of services as the implementation of capabilities, classified as Federation Services, Participant Agent Services, and Value Creation Services. "Technical services" within the meaning of the Blueprint may not be the object of a contractual relationship when they are considered ancillary to the main purpose of a contract — e.g. technical functionalities necessary to provide access to a data space. Alternatively, they may be mentioned as part of the contractual obligations that the Data Governance Authority commits to, with clauses including provisions typical for Service-Level Agreements. A different scenario is services that are externalised to third parties.

**Data product contract.** A legal agreement that sets out the terms and conditions under which a data product is made available. In most cases data providers will make data available subject to specific terms and conditions; alternatively the data space may include a list of possible data licences in the general terms and conditions from which either the data provider or data recipient can choose. Some clauses to be included: identification of the data; acceptable purposes (purposes for which the data product can be used); restrictions of use for the data product; usage rights of the data product; termination of the contract; terms related to the use of derived material; fees and monetisation; data security; and clauses related to third-party rights (data protection, intellectual property rights, confidentiality). The source cites the **Data Usage Agreement** by GAIA-X as a concrete example with instructions on technical implementation.

*Legal aspects to consider when designing data products:*

- **Lawful source of data/content** — where the data represents copyright-protected content, the data space may under certain circumstances be liable for copyright infringement. Contractual clauses addressing this include warranties (e.g. the data provider warrants that there are no third-party claims over the data and that all due diligence procedures have been carried out to verify this), allocation of liability, and indemnities. These may be included in the accession agreement when vetting new participants or in the data product contract itself.
- **Processing of personal data** — cross-referenced to the Regulatory Compliance building block.
- **Compulsory B2B data-sharing contracts** — if data holders make data available in accordance with an obligation under EU law, the terms and conditions must be **FRAND (fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory)** (**Chapter 3 Data Act**), which also regulates compensation, dispute settlement provisions and technical protection measures.
- **Unilateral-imposed B2B (private) data sharing** — unilateral terms and conditions regarding data availability are non-binding to the extent that they are deemed unfair (**Chapter 4 Data Act**); to determine whether a clause is unfair, refer to **Art 13**.
- **Applicability of sectoral regulation** — **Chapter 2 of the Data Act** for IoT data, or the need for compliance with horizontal regulations (e.g. competition law).
- **Jurisdiction and applicable law** — each contract should include clauses that regulate choice of law and choice of forum. *Jurisdiction*: there is a free choice of jurisdiction unless the contract is deemed null and void under the law of the selected state, or unless the contract is concluded with a consumer and the commercial party conducts activities in the consumer's domicile; failing that, jurisdiction is determined by law (see **Brussels Ibis Regulation**). *Applicable law*: the parties are free to determine the law governing their contract; in the case of contracts with consumers, the choice of law will not derogate from the mandatory consumer protection provisions in the state where the consumer has their habitual residence; in the absence of a choice, the applicable law will be determined by legislation (see **Rome I Regulation**). It may be desirable to harmonise matters of jurisdiction and applicable law across all agreements within the contractual framework.
- **Smart contracts** — a smart contract does not presuppose the existence of a legally valid and enforceable contract; rather, it enables the technical enforceability of contracts and can be used to formalise legal agreements, contract negotiation, and execution. It can also serve as a technical protection measure to prevent unauthorised access to data. When smart contracts are used in the context of executing an agreement to make data accessible, the Data Act imposes a set of essential requirements that the data provider must comply with (**Data Act Art 36**). In some jurisdictions the deployment of a smart contract may suffice to support the existence of a legal relationship. It is advisable for the sake of legal certainty to always generate a separate document written in natural, concise and simple language that outlines the main elements of the contract and the mutual obligations it supports. If smart contracts are deployed in a data space, they must comply with the essential requirements of **Art 33 of the Data Act** aimed at enabling interoperability of tools for automating the execution of data-sharing agreements (see in particular **Art 33(1)(d)**), as well as **Art 36 of the Data Act**, which mandates a series of essential requirements for smart contracts (e.g. robustness and access control, safe termination and interruption).

**Services agreements.** Key elements encompassed in Services Agreements include: **Scope of Services** (nature, purpose, and boundaries of the services provided, including the types of data to be processed, functional capabilities, interface standards, and technical interoperability requirements); **Roles and Responsibilities** (of service providers, data consumers, and intermediaries, tied to operational duties including service provisioning, compliance enforcement, and auditability); **Access and Usage Rights** (authorized access procedures and use limitations, aligned with predefined policies and legal frameworks such as the GDPR and the Data Governance Act); **Service Level Agreements (SLAs)** (performance metrics such as availability, latency, throughput, maintenance windows, incident handling procedures, and service restoration times, plus remedies or compensations where service levels are not met); **Data Protection and Security** (technical safeguards such as encryption, secure API gateways, identity verification, and organizational safeguards, plus security incident response, data breach notifications, and continuous monitoring); **Compliance and Liability** (audit rights, obligations for third-party subcontractors, mechanisms for dispute resolution, and any limitations or exclusions of liability); and **Term, Termination, and Amendments** (term and duration, renewal or extension clauses, termination rights, and formal procedures for modifying the agreement). In some cases the conditions under which a service is provided may be directly regulated in the General Terms and Conditions.

#### The classification of contractual clauses and licences

The source describes the **List of Contractual Clauses** as a key supporting tool, compiling existing standard clauses relevant to data sharing and classifying them according to their main characteristics — such as commercial or non-commercial use, attribution requirements and permissions to modify the data. Licences are colour-coded to indicate their relative position along a spectrum of openness, following the broad framework developed by the Reusable Data Project:

- **Permissive** (green): licenses that allow modification and redistribution for both commercial and non-commercial purposes without substantial restrictions or obligations.
- **Copyleft** (blue): licenses that permit re-use but require derivative works to be distributed under the same license.
- **Private pool** (yellow): licenses that require data users to contribute their own data to access the licensed data or to make derivative data available only within a specific community of users.
- **Restrictive** (red): licenses that grant only limited rights for access or re-use, prohibiting modification or imposing specific conditions on how the data may be used.

The source also names contract-automation and licence-selection tooling: the **Commission Recommendation on non-binding model contractual terms on data access and use and non-binding standard contractual clauses for cloud computing contracts**; **Prometheus-X (Contract)** for data contract generation; the **ELRA License Wizard**; and the **Licence Selector**.

## Open questions

Ambiguities, gaps and contradictions found in the source for this pane:

- **Two pages have no breadcrumb trail.** *Business Model* and *Example Canvases* carry no breadcrumb path in the source's index and their page headings are slugs (`business-model`, `example-canvases`). Their placement relative to the three Building Block families (Business / Governance / Legal) cannot be determined from the source; the names used on this page are those given by the pane navigation. Whether *Example Canvases* is a sub-page of *Business Model* or a sibling is likewise undetermined.
- **Duplicate section numbering in Regulatory Compliance.** Both the *Data Act* and the *European Health Data Space Regulation* sub-sections are numbered `3.2.1`.
- **Empty table cell.** In *Types of data (Data as a Trigger)*, the row for **Non-personal data** has no description or impact assessment at all.
- **Self-contradiction on taxonomy completeness.** *Types of data (Data as a Trigger)* describes its overview as aiming to be "an exhaustive taxonomy of data types" while stating in the same sentence that the classification "remains a work in progress".
- **Three incompatible definitions of "data product".** The CEN CENELEC CWA definition quoted in *Data Space Offerings*, and two further formulations in *Contractual Framework* §3.2 and §3.4.2.1, differ in what is packaged (data and metadata vs. resources including value creation services) and in whether machine-readability is part of the definition. All three are attributed to the *Data Space Offering* building block.
- **"DSA" used ambiguously.** In *Contractual Framework* §3.4.3, "the DSA defines contractual lifecycle elements" appears from context to mean the services agreement, not the Digital Services Act, which is referenced by the same acronym elsewhere in the pane.
- **RegTech categorisation marked incomplete.** *Regulatory Compliance* §3.4.2.1 is explicitly labelled "(work in progress)". Two rows of its Toolbox table (Gaia-X Registry, Gaia-X Compliance Service) have no value in the "Relevant Building Block" column, and one row's value carries a question mark ("Data Space Offerings?").
- **Unresolved legal questions raised but not answered.** Whether a data space as a whole can be considered a critical infrastructure under NIS-2, and whether a data space can be considered a "provider of online marketplace" under Annex II of NIS-2, are both raised and explicitly left open ("such consideration remains open to explore").
- **Responsibilities stated without modal verbs.** The DSGA / service provider responsibilities table in *Intermediaries and Operators* §3.2.3 is written in the imperative with no `must`/`should`. It is recorded here as `should`; the source's normative force is not stated.
- **Two headings numbered `4.4` in Use Case Development.** Both "Implementing use cases" and "Continuous improvement process" carry the number `4.4`.
- **Section 3.5 of Regulatory Compliance refers to figures.** The Regulatory Compliance Flowcharts (Figures 7–10) are images in the source; their content is not available in text form, so only the surrounding prose about their purpose and limits is rendered here.
